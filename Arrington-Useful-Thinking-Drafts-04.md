## A11. A Profitable Job Is Not Necessarily Good Business

**Status:** Ready. Source: supplied directly by Tom, this session, as a working draft ("So building a post, thoughts:"), then finalized after two pressure-test points were resolved (a wording slip, "bells and braces" → "belt and braces," confirmed by Tom; and a double-ending merged into one, since every other piece in the library lands on a single closing line).

**Real incident:** A shipping company operating from Falmouth Docks needed its Romanian crew transported to and from Luton Airport for shift changes — profitable, high-mileage-efficiency work, but with payment terms stretching up to ten months, at times leaving tens of thousands of pounds owed. Contrasted against FTSE 250 clients with similar delays but a different cause (process failure, not cash shortage), handled differently (legal threat, which worked) versus the shipping company (honest conversation and a payment pause, which also worked, because the leverage that fits one debtor doesn't fit another).

**Commercial lesson (single):** A profitable job is not necessarily good business if you have to finance the customer for nearly a year.

**Why owners get this wrong** *(suggested framing, not a sourced Tom quote)*: a profitable-looking job is easy to keep saying yes to, because the loss doesn't show up as a loss — it shows up as slow cash, which feels like an administrative problem rather than a decision that needs revisiting.

**What changed Tom's thinking:** working out that the two debtors needed opposite responses — legal pressure for the FTSE 250 companies, relationship-based honesty for the shipping company — because the same lever doesn't move every kind of non-payment.

**Commercial Gaps Review categories (unverified — no taxonomy located):** cash flow and credit risk; customer relationship management; knowing when to walk away from profitable-looking work.

**Suggested internal links:** none obvious among current Ready pieces — first piece in the library specifically about credit risk and customer selection.

**Evidence source:** Tom, direct, this session. Not sourced from Drive.

**Final website-ready copy (Useful Thinking):**

We thought we had landed a licence to print money.

A shipping company operating from Falmouth Docks needed its Romanian crew transported to and from Luton Airport for shift changes.

Sometimes we would have one vehicle going up and another coming back. Other times it could be four going up and three returning. Long-distance fares, passengers in both directions and very little empty mileage. On paper, it was brilliant work.

The journeys were profitable. Getting paid for them was another matter.

We paid the drivers and bought the fuel immediately, but could wait up to ten months for the invoices to be settled. At times, the company owed us tens of thousands of pounds.

In the earlier years of the business, when cash was tighter, that meant using our own money to finance someone else's operation while waiting nearly a year to receive our profit.

The people we dealt with were not deliberately withholding payment. The shipping company was waiting to be paid itself and the money simply was not there. That made the situation understandable, but it did not make the risk disappear.

Eventually, we had to refuse further work until the balance came down.

Interestingly, we were also owed money for similar periods by FTSE 250 companies, but I handled those differently. Their delays were usually caused by oversight or poor internal processes rather than a lack of cash. I continued accepting the work but made it clear that, as a smaller operator being forced to carry their debt, I would take legal action if necessary.

That always resulted in payment.

With the smaller shipping company, a legal threat would not have made money appear. It was more likely to damage a valuable relationship with the people we dealt with every day. Instead, I spoke honestly with the CEO about the pressure it was putting on our business and followed it up in writing.

The balance was always paid and the relationship survived.

You can put all the belt and braces you like into your payment terms. They give you rights, but they do not put money into a customer's bank account. What protected us was knowing the people involved, understanding why we had not been paid, and being prepared to stop extending more credit when the risk became too high.

A profitable job is not necessarily good business if you have to finance the customer for nearly a year.

**Real incident:** Took over a taxi company running out-of-town work on a first-in-first-out basis. Drivers gamed each successive rule — arriving early and leaving before evening demand under FIFO, front-loading a mandated 8-hour shift, and so on — through four successive rule changes (minimum shift length, predetermined first/second car, evening-list access tied to availability), none of which was a permanent fix. Set against nearly 20 years of demand consistently exceeding driver supply, worsened later by Covid-era staffing shortages.

**Commercial lesson (single):** Every rule changes behaviour. Sometimes the behaviour it creates becomes the next problem the business has to solve.

**Why owners get this wrong** *(suggested framing, not a sourced Tom quote)*: a new rule feels like a fix because it solves the problem you can see, but it doesn't remove the underlying pressure driving the behaviour — it just redirects it somewhere you haven't noticed yet.

**What changed Tom's thinking:** watching each fix produce a new, different gap rather than closing the original one, across four iterations — enough repetition to conclude there wasn't a perfect rule waiting to be found, only a balance to keep re-watching.

**Commercial Gaps Review categories (unverified):** incentive design and unintended consequences; staff structure and morale; operational capacity planning.

**Suggested internal links:** none obvious among current Ready pieces — first piece specifically about rule/incentive design rather than a single decision or relationship.

**Evidence source:** Tom, direct, this session. Not sourced from Drive.

**Final website-ready copy (Useful Thinking, subheadings removed, analogy removed per above):**

Every Rule Changes Behaviour

When we took over the taxi company, out of town work operated on a simple first in, first out basis.

It appeared fair. The earlier a driver started, the higher they moved up the list and the better their chance of receiving a valuable long distance journey.

The drivers soon responded exactly as the system encouraged them to.

More began arriving early, including drivers we did not actually need at that time of day. They completed a few good jobs, reached the end of their shift and went home while much of the day remained.

The system distributed the work fairly between individual drivers. It did not put enough cars on the road when the business and its customers needed them.

We introduced a minimum eight hour shift to stop drivers arriving early, completing a couple of profitable jobs and disappearing.

That changed the behaviour, but not in the way we needed.

Some drivers began starting outrageously early. They still completed the required eight hours, but could then finish before the evening demand arrived.

By 5pm, during the gap between the daytime and evening drivers, we could barely have any cars available.

The rule was being followed. The outcome was still wrong.

We then predetermined which drivers would be the first and second cars. That guaranteed the essential early coverage. Everyone else could decide when to start, provided they completed the minimum shift.

When the evening shortage continued, access to the out of town list became dependent on drivers being available by a particular time.

It was presented as an incentive, although in truth it was probably a stick disguised as a carrot. Drivers who were not available when the business needed them would miss the opportunity to receive the more desirable work.

It helped, but it never completely solved the problem.

The same thing happens with any rule or incentive that affects somebody's earnings. People naturally adjust their behaviour around it. That does not make them dishonest or difficult. It means the system is producing the behaviour it rewards.

The mistake is assuming that because a rule sounds sensible, the combined result will also be sensible. A system can treat each person fairly while producing a poor result for the business.

The obvious response to every new problem is another rule. That eventually creates a different kind of damage.

Too little structure allowed drivers to maximise their immediate earnings while leaving the business short of cars later in the day. Too much structure would have damaged morale, removed useful independence and made good drivers feel they were being micromanaged.

That mattered commercially. Driver retention was already difficult. Making the working environment unnecessarily restrictive would only have made it harder.

The business needed enough control to protect customer coverage, but enough freedom for drivers to govern themselves. Finding that balance was not a one off decision. Each adjustment changed behaviour and had to be watched. Covid and the staffing shortages that followed made it harder again.

Across nearly 20 years, we only had enough drivers for brief periods. Demand almost always exceeded supply, even during recessions. That was both the joy and the stress of owning a busy business. There was always work available. The difficulty was having enough capacity in the right place at the right time. No single rule could remove that underlying pressure.

Good management was not about discovering a perfect system and leaving it alone. It meant watching what people actually did, understanding why they did it and deciding whether the next change would improve the overall result or simply move the problem somewhere else.

Every rule changes behaviour.

Sometimes the behaviour it creates becomes the next problem the business has to solve.
