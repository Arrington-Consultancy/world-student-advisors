# Editorial Ranking — Ten Ready Pieces, and the Five Highest-Value Missing Stories

Editor/interviewer pass, not a research pass. No Drive searching done to produce this — everything below is judgement applied to evidence already gathered. CV framing and the "is Arrington Consultancy the primary venture" question are deliberately excluded, per instruction — out of scope for content quality.

---

## Ranking

Tested against: (1) would this genuinely change how an owner thinks, (2) is the lesson earned by the evidence, (3) is it distinct from the others, (4) does it sound like Tom rather than polished business writing, (5) would it survive a hard cut to twenty pieces.

### Publish first

**1. Being Certain Isn't the Same as Being Right.** Clears all five without qualification. Full incident, clean resolution, distinct territory (false certainty, wrongful accusation), and the driest, most Tom-sounding line in the whole set ("I've occasionally even been right, although that depends on whether you ask my wife"). The gold-standard sourcing of the set — pressure-tested, approved copy, nothing reconstructed.

**2. The Customer Who Messaged Me at 4am.** Same standard of sourcing. Distinct from the owner-dependency territory of other pieces because the access problem is customer-side, not staff-side. The Marks & Spencer line does real work — it's funny and it's the moment the lesson lands, not decoration.

**3. You Don't Get to Decide When You've Made Things Right.** The most quietly uncomfortable lesson in the set — most "accountability" content tells owners what to do; this one tells them what they don't get to control. Real incident, real years-later resolution, earns its ending.

**7. The Tightrope Between Staff Loyalty and Damage Control.** Strongest people-management piece. "98% of the job was done well" is the kind of specific, ungenerous-to-himself detail that only comes from a real incident. "Trust me, I have tried" is exactly the register the whole project has been chasing.

**9. The Reverse Economy of Scale.** Flagged honestly rather than passed silently: this is the one piece in the top tier that leans closer to a distilled thesis than a scene-by-scene story — no single dramatised moment, more articulated principle than anecdote. It stays in the top tier because it's the most quotable, most central idea in the whole library, independently confirmed by Tom's own working notes as the strongest recurring concept, and still carries real grounding (his own experience plus an actual conversation). But it's the one where test 4 is genuinely closest to failing, and that's worth knowing rather than glossing over.

### Strong but later

**4. The Most Expensive Date I Ever Had.** Real, honest, distinct (the only piece about backing someone else's venture rather than running Tom's own). Held back from the top tier because it's less central to Arrington's core audience — owners struggling with structure and dependency in a business they already run — than to a broader "risk and judgement" theme. Good range-extender, not a top-five hit.

**5. The Real Failure Was Letting That Much Risk Sit in One Place.** The lesson is real and distinct from piece 3 — structural prevention versus post-mistake accountability. But it shares one incident with piece 3, and in a tight twenty-piece library, two pieces from the same story sitting near each other risks reading as stretching one incident into two, even where the underlying logic for the split holds up. Strong content, secondary placement.

**10. Staff Retention Is Transactional Underneath It All.** Genuinely useful, genuinely Tom's observation — but structurally the weakest of the ten under scrutiny. It's the only Ready piece with no single incident behind it (an observed pattern, correctly kept in that voice), which means it reads closer to reflective essay than story, and it sits close enough to piece 7's territory (staff loyalty) that if the library only had room for one staff-loyalty piece, 7 would win on concreteness alone. Worth keeping, not worth ranking as a flagship.

### Needs another Tom pass

**6. I Handed My Own Judgement to Somebody Else.** The lesson is real and the two verbatim lines are solid, but under honest scrutiny this is the piece I did the most reconstruction on — several sentences were rebuilt from the Story Bank's own editing notes about what needed fixing, not pulled from a finished Tom draft, and there's no recorded outcome for the garage itself. It would be measurably stronger with Tom's own final wording and a real ending rather than stopping cleanly on the lesson line because that's where the evidence runs out.

**8. The Closest I've Come to Being Kicked Out of a Business Was in the First Hour.** The incident and its publication are independently confirmed, and the lesson is genuinely valuable — the only piece where Tom is the outsider, not the owner, which gives the library range. But no document labelled "final" was ever found; the wording is reconstructed from the fullest surviving archived draft. Before this sits in the same tier as pieces 1 and 2, it deserves the same confirmation they got.

### Cut

None. Everything that reached Ready status went through the evidence-first process and is real — a cut here would mean discarding something true because it's merely good rather than excellent, and nothing currently in the set is weak enough to justify that. The honest finding from this pass isn't "cut," it's "don't let 4, 5 and 10 crowd out sharper material later, and don't let 6 or 8 stay in the library with reconstructed wording standing in for Tom's own."

## The five highest-value missing stories

Drawn entirely from gaps already identified in earlier rounds — nothing new searched for. Ranked by a mix of how strong the payoff looks and how little is actually missing.

### 1. The Leo birth story — smallest gap, highest existing investment
Everything is already evidenced except one fact. The lesson is locked (owner dependency, operational preparation, not sentiment).

**Question:** What specifically changed operationally after this — a briefing change, an escalation rule, someone else given standing authority to make that call, or something else?

### 2. The £2,500 cash buffer
The strongest-corroborated unextracted incident in the whole Brain — the underlying instinct ("don't trust the first version of a problem") is independently stated three separate times across three different documents. Only one line of raw material exists.

**Questions:**
1. What business, and roughly when?
2. What was the buffer actually protecting against — what rushed fix did it let you avoid making?
3. What changed, concretely, because you had that time rather than reacting immediately?

### 3. The employee who left for a competitor
Already confirmed real and published (7 July 2026, Facebook and Instagram) — this is the fastest possible route to an eleventh Ready piece, since only the full account is missing, not the underlying fact of the incident. The existing paraphrase says people who've worked inside the business see angles the owner can't.

**Questions:**
1. Who left and roughly when (can be anonymised if you'd rather)?
2. What did they see or say, on the way out or afterwards, that you hadn't seen yourself?
3. What did you actually change as a result of hearing it?

### 4. The staff complaint that needed a cooling-off period
Named specifically in the Useful Thinking Bank as one of four proof points behind "you don't trust the first version of a problem," alongside the cash buffer. Distinct territory from the cash buffer — this one is about people and fairness under pressure, not cash and timing.

**Questions:**
1. What was the complaint, and roughly when?
2. What would you have done if you'd reacted the moment it landed, versus what you actually did after waiting?
3. What turned out to be different from the first version of the story?

### 5. The serious allegation that needed evidence before action
Also named in the Useful Thinking Bank, alongside the cash buffer and the cooling-off complaint. Flagging before asking: this sounds like it may involve a real person and a genuinely serious matter, so treat the questions as a starting point — generalise or leave out anything you'd rather not have on record, and say so rather than skip the story entirely if the detail is sensitive.

**Questions:**
1. In general terms, what was the allegation and against whom (a role is fine if a name isn't appropriate)?
2. What evidence-gathering actually happened before you acted?
3. What would have gone wrong if you'd acted on the first version instead of waiting for evidence?
