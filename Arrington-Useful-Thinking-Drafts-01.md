## A1. Being Certain Isn't the Same as Being Right

**Status:** Ready. Source: Story Bank Entry 001, pressure tested and approved.

I've been wrong millions of times.

I've occasionally even been right, although that depends on whether you ask my wife.

But only a handful of times in my life have I been so completely certain about something that I would have bet my life on it, only to be proved wrong. This is the business one.

I rang the office one day and couldn't get through. I tried again from a different number. Still nothing. I checked the CCTV while it was ringing and saw the member of staff just sitting there.

He'd previously told me he didn't use the loudspeaker, so I'd ruled that out. No handset in his hand meant one thing to me: he was ignoring the phone.

I confronted him. He denied it. I was so certain I'd seen it with my own eyes that I called him a liar.

He still denied it, so I checked the call logs. He'd been on another call at the exact moment mine came in — on loudspeaker, exactly what he'd told me he didn't do.

I'd been wrong about the lie. He had broken a rule and concealed it, but I had accused him of something he hadn't done. Neither of us came out of it clean, but only one of us had been called a liar for something he hadn't done.

I apologised straight away.

It's one of the first things I tell new managers when they're considering disciplining a member of staff: never flat out call someone a liar.

**Being certain doesn't make you right. Listen, gather the evidence and leave room for the possibility that you're wrong.**

Sometimes even your own eyes give you the wrong answer.

---

**Why owners get this wrong** *(suggested framing — not a sourced Tom quote, flagged as such)*: owners who built the business themselves usually trust their own read of a situation, because for years their instinct was right often enough to be trusted. That's exactly what makes premature certainty dangerous — the track record that makes an owner confident is the same thing that stops them checking.

**What changed Tom's thinking:** finding the call logs and being proved wrong by his own evidence-gathering, not by the staff member's word.

**Commercial Gaps Review categories (unverified):** decision-making under pressure; staff discipline and trust.

## A2. The Customer Who Messaged Me at 4am

**Status:** Ready. Source: Story Bank Entry 003 (4am entry), pressure tested and approved.

I was sound asleep over the festive period when a customer I knew to maybe say hello to felt it was acceptable to message me at 4am, ranting about a late taxi.

My warped sense of what was normal meant I replied, then rang the office to sort it.

The following morning my wife asked who had been messaging me at that hour. I explained. She looked at me and said: "How can anyone possibly think that's acceptable? What if I contacted the CEO of Marks and Spencer with a complaint?"

My wife has an art of hyperbolising the extent of a breach of boundaries. She was also completely right.

At the time we were handling around 10,000 customers a week and I had spent years making the business genuinely responsive, but somewhere along the way I had confused caring about customers with being personally available to them at any hour.

Over twenty years the defining change was communication technology. The landline only ever got used for a genuine someone is on fire emergency. The mobile made contact feel somehow less intrusive to the person sending it, even when it wasn't.

Once people know they can reach you directly, it is very difficult to walk that back. The same applied to staff. Genuine emergencies, yes. A gripe that could wait until Monday morning belonged in the right place at the right time, not landing on me whenever somebody felt like having it.

It took a 4am message over Christmas and my wife's reaction the next morning to make me draw the line properly. We still smile about the Marks and Spencer line.

**She was right. I should have done it sooner.**

---

**Why owners get this wrong** *(suggested framing, not a sourced Tom quote)*: responsiveness is genuinely a strength in a small business, which is exactly the trap — owners proud of never dropping the ball can't easily tell the difference between handling things properly and being reachable by anyone, at any hour, until someone outside the business points out how strange that actually looks.

**What changed Tom's thinking:** his wife's reaction the next morning — sourced directly.

**Commercial Gaps Review categories (unverified):** owner boundaries; customer access; owner dependency.

**Suggested internal links:** Owner Dependency Quiz. A future link to the Leo birth piece once it's finished — both are owner-boundary stories, but they are not to be merged or treated as one lesson.

## A3. You Don't Get to Decide When You've Made Things Right

**Status:** Ready — new discovery this session. Source: "APPROVED POST — £120,000 Account Lost Over One Airport Transfer" (10 Social Media > Social Media > Social Posting Schedule). Marked "Approved, scheduled" for LinkedIn and Facebook, 23 June 2026. This was not in the Story Bank and was missed in the original audit — found this session by searching further into the social content folders per your instruction to keep working the evidence.

I lost a £120,000 a year account when I was 26 because of one drive to an airport.

We had made provisions for bad traffic, but not quite enough. The passenger was late and, funnily enough, the plane did not wait. The fault was ours and ours only.

I covered the cost to the company, apologised properly and spoke directly to the MD. At the time, I genuinely believed I had fixed it. I had taken responsibility, put my hand in my pocket and done everything I thought a decent business owner was supposed to do. I had read the book and everything.

He accepted the apology. He accepted the compensation. Then he ended the relationship.

At the time I felt betrayed. Looking back, he was not punishing me. He was protecting his customers, his business and the food he put on his family's table. I was irrelevant in that picture.

Business is not a social club. You can be friendly, but you are often not friends.

You can make the right moves afterwards. You can apologise, compensate and dance all the right dances. What you do not get to decide is what happens next. If you get it wrong in business, it is not your decision what the punishment should be. The offender is not the judge and jury.

There were staff involved and internal mistakes that contributed to what happened. I could have pointed the finger and badly wanted to. I suspect some people were waiting for me to. Even at 26, I knew that was nonsense. If somebody on minimum wage can make a mistake that costs your business hundreds of thousands of pounds, that is not really a staff problem. That is a management problem and, in my case, a Tom problem.

The real failure was not the late airport run. The real failure was allowing that much risk to sit in one place. After that, every account booking had to be confirmed by email and signed off. We built paper trails where there had not been any — not because we wanted more administration, but because I had learned what a missing process could cost.

A few years later, the same MD was let down by one of our competitors and gave us another chance. The original incident was never mentioned. It did not need to be. We both knew what had happened, and he knew it would never happen again.

**If I could speak to myself at 26, I would tell him it hurts now, and rightly so. But one day you will thank him for it.**

---

**Locked commercial lesson (per the source document's own "Core lesson" field):** you do not get to decide when you have made things right. Accountability means accepting the other side's timeline and decision, not your own.

**Flag for your decision — do not treat as resolved:** this story also carries a second, strong, distinct commercial thread — "the real failure was allowing that much risk to sit in one place," i.e. a junior person's honest mistake shouldn't be able to cost the business hundreds of thousands, and that's a process/structure failure, not a people failure. That's close to real evidence for **concept 7, "The Problem Usually Isn't the Person"** — but it's a second lesson from one story, the same situation as the CCTV restriction. I have not built it as a second article. If you want to approve reusing this story for that second, distinct lesson, say so and I'll draft it separately.

**Commercial Gaps Review categories (unverified):** accountability and consequences; risk concentration / process.

**Suggested internal links:** Owner Dependency Quiz (the "management problem, in my case a Tom problem" line is a direct fit).

**Potential follow-up articles:** "The Problem Usually Isn't the Person" — pending your explicit approval, per above.
