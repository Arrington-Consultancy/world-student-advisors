## A4. The Most Expensive Date I Ever Had (£6,000 Dating Website)

**Status:** Ready — new discovery this session. Source: "APPROVED POST — £6,000 Dating Website Investment" (Social Media root folder), marked "Approved, not yet scheduled." Not in the Story Bank. Found by searching the exact "APPROVED POST" title pattern that surfaced the £120,000 story last round — a useful search method, now exhausted for that pattern (only two such documents exist).

In 2009 I put about £6,000 into a dating website. It was nearly ahead of its time, but it turns out the second person to invent the wheel is not laughing all the way to the bank.

It was not a new idea scribbled on the back of a napkin. It was an existing site with a bit of traction, enough to make it feel like there might be something there if it had backing, a proper rebuild and the right push behind it.

I also had a friend behind it who believed in it completely.

Most friendship groups have someone like him. Always convinced by the next business, the next investment, the next opportunity. Always convinced this one is the big one. The enthusiasm is contagious because they are not lying to you. They genuinely believe it.

At the time, I could see why he believed it. The timing felt right, other sites were starting to prove the space was real, and it did not feel like a ridiculous leap. It felt like a punt with an upside. A manageable risk, not one that could break me, but one that had a tiny chance to make me.

The problem was that we thought we were buying a vision we had created. In reality, we were trying to build a technology business without owning the technology.

We had the vision, we were full of hope and we believed that if we built it properly, people would come. But having a vision you can't create yourself is incredibly frustrating. We couldn't speak web designer and the web designers couldn't speak enthusiastic entrepreneur.

We were promised the holy grail by the website designers. What we ended up with was a long way from anything remotely like our vision.

The site got built, but not in the way we imagined. We tried to advertise it and push it, but over time it became obvious it was never going to become self sufficient. There was no dramatic ending. It just slowly drifted into nothing.

I lost the money, but I have no regrets. It was a brilliant education. It took me into a world I did not understand, showed me how easily enthusiasm can pull you into something, and taught me that a decent idea is not the same as the ability to execute it.

My friend moved on to the next thing, then the next, then eventually crypto. I still listen. I still like the energy. I just learned to become a supportive observer rather than the person writing the cheque.

Looking back, I realise I was investing in two things. The website and the enthusiasm behind it.

The website failed. The enthusiasm never did.

**You invest in more than just the idea — you invest in the person and their belief. Know which one you're actually backing.**

**Why owners get this wrong** *(suggested framing, not a sourced Tom quote)*: enthusiasm is genuinely persuasive, and it's easy to mistake someone else's conviction for your own due diligence — the excitement does the work that a proper look at execution capability should have done.

**What changed Tom's thinking:** watching the site drift into nothing despite the vision being intact — proof that having a vision and being able to build it are two different skills.

## A5. The Real Failure Was Letting That Much Risk Sit in One Place

**Status:** Ready — second article from the £120,000 account story, per your decision 2. Explanation below, as required, before the draft.

**Why this deserves to exist as a separate piece, not a restatement of A3:**

A3 ("You Don't Get to Decide When You've Made Things Right") answers a question about the aftermath of a mistake — how an owner should behave once something has gone wrong, and what they're entitled to expect back from the other side. Its evidence is the apology, the compensation, the MD ending the relationship anyway, and the delayed second chance.

This piece answers a different, earlier question — how a business should be built so that one ordinary person's ordinary mistake can't cost it hundreds of thousands of pounds in the first place. Its evidence is a separate passage in the same document: "if somebody on minimum wage can make a mistake that costs your business hundreds of thousands of pounds, that is not really a staff problem... the real failure was allowing that much risk to sit in one place," followed by the concrete process change — every account booking confirmed by email and signed off, paper trails built where none existed.

A reader could act fully on one of these without the other. Owning your consequences (A3) doesn't tell you anything about how to design your business so the consequences are smaller next time (A5), and fixing the process (A5) doesn't tell you how to behave the next time you're the one who's failed someone. That's the test for two commercial points, not one stretched thin — and it matches the Story Bank's own rule that when two lessons exist in one incident, they become two entries, not one.

Draft:

I lost a £120,000 a year account when I was 26 because of one drive to an airport.

We had made provisions for bad traffic, but not quite enough. The passenger was late and the plane did not wait. I covered the cost, apologised properly and did what I thought a decent business owner was supposed to do. It did not save the account, and that is a different story.

Here is the part I keep coming back to. There were staff involved and internal mistakes that contributed to what happened. I could have pointed the finger and badly wanted to. Even at 26, I knew that was nonsense.

If somebody on minimum wage can make a mistake that costs your business hundreds of thousands of pounds, that is not really a staff problem. That is a management problem, and in my case, a Tom problem.

The real failure was not the late airport run. The real failure was allowing that much risk to sit in one place, resting on one driver, one journey, one afternoon, with nothing else standing behind it.

Someone earning minimum wage deserves to be protected from errors that can make or break a business. Not because they cannot be trusted, but because no single ordinary person should be able to do that much damage on an ordinary day.

After that, every account booking had to be confirmed by email. Every account booking had to be signed off. We built paper trails where there had not been any.

**Not because we wanted more administration. Because I had learned what a missing process could cost.**

I was 24 when I agreed to open a garage I never really believed in.

An older business partner and a mechanic who wanted to leave offshore work put the idea to me. I didn't trust the mechanic's work ethic and I wasn't comfortable with the direction, but I didn't feel I had the authority to challenge people older and more experienced than me.

At 24, almost everyone in business felt older and more experienced than me. They were excited and certain, and I didn't want to be the young person who said no. They must know what they were doing — look how sure they were. What did I know? I'd graduated six months ago.

I could see it in their eyes: this was how we were going to make our millions.

**I can live with my own mistakes and errors, but I do not ever have to suffer because I handed my judgement to somebody else.**
